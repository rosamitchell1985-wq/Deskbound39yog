Seated cat-cow

Sit forward on the chair, feet flat, hands on your knees. Round your back and drop your head, then arch and lift your chest. Slow. About eight rounds in a minute.

Position: Seated + standing. Equipment: none. Sound is off until you turn it on.

On any move with a left and a right, give each side thirty seconds. The timer does not split sides for you — you'd spend the minute watching the screen instead of your body.

This is mobility work that happens to be yoga

Nobody here is going to ask you to breathe into anything. The reason these shapes come from yoga is that yoga has a large library of positions you can get into without equipment, in a small space, wearing whatever you have on. That is a practical advantage when your gap is the eleven minutes between a design review and a standup, and the only floor space you have is the strip between your chair and the wall.

The problem is simple and mechanical. A seated workday puts your hips in about 90 degrees of flexion for eight hours, your low back in a long sustained round, your shoulder blades pulled forward around a keyboard, and your head a few inches in front of your shoulders looking at a screen that is almost certainly too low. Tissue adapts to the position you spend the most time in. Hold any of those long enough and the tissue gets good at that position and bad at the opposite one. That is the stiffness you feel at 3 PM and the wince you get when you finally stand up.

Movement breaks work on that in the least glamorous way possible: they interrupt the position. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration's workstation guidance makes the same point in flatter language — the fix for a static posture is not a better static posture but more changes of position through the day. Five minutes of deliberate movement does not undo eight hours of sitting. It breaks the eight hours into pieces, which is a different and more achievable goal.

What you should expect, honestly

Most people notice something within the session — the neck moves further, the low back feels less locked, standing up doesn't produce that involuntary noise. That is a change in how the area feels and moves right now. It is not a structural fix and it does not treat any diagnosis.

What builds over weeks is different and less dramatic. You start standing up more often because the reset gives you a reason to. You stop finishing Thursdays with a headache from your traps. You notice you have been leaning your head toward the monitor and you stop, because chin tucks made you aware of where your head actually sits. That awareness is most of the value and nobody advertises it, because it is boring.

The rules for all four routines

  • Work at about a 4 out of 10. A clear stretch. If you are grimacing, you have gone past useful and into a fight your nervous system will win.
  • Sharp is a stop sign. Dull and stretchy is fine. Sharp, electric, or anything that runs down a limb means come out of the move and skip it.
  • Thirty seconds a side. On any move with a left and a right, split the minute evenly. Do the tight side second if you want to feel the difference.
  • Shoes off for the standing moves if you can. Hard-soled dress shoes change how you balance in the forward fold and the hip flexor stretch. Not required, just easier.
  • Breathe like a normal person. No counted breathing, no technique. If you are holding your breath, you are pushing too hard.

When to run it

Two resets a day covers most people: one late morning, one at about 3 PM. If you are only going to do one, do the afternoon one. Stiffness reliably peaks in the last third of the workday, and that is also when your setup quietly gets worse — you slide forward in the chair, the laptop creeps closer, your feet end up hooked around the chair legs.

The other thing worth doing costs nothing: get up roughly every half hour, even for twenty seconds. Refill the water. Take the call standing. Walk to the printer that is deliberately far away. The routines on this site exist because a structured five minutes is easier to actually do than a vague instruction to move more, but the frequent standing is doing at least as much work as the routine is.

If your day is wall-to-wall meetings, the seated moves — cat-cow, chin tucks, figure-4 — are all camera-safe from the waist up or entirely invisible below the desk. Nobody on the call is going to notice you doing chin tucks. They will notice a doorway chest opener, so save that one.

Questions people actually ask

Will 5 minutes actually help?

Five minutes will not rebuild your back. What it does is interrupt the position that is aggravating it, and it does that on a schedule you will actually keep. That is the trade: a small dose you repeat beats a large dose you skip.

Think of it the way you'd think of standing up during a long flight. One lap of the aisle doesn't fix anything either, but the flight feels very different depending on whether you took it.

I'm not flexible. Does that matter?

No, and it is close to irrelevant here. Nothing on this site requires you to touch your toes, sit on the floor, or hold anything for a long time. Bend your knees as much as you want in the forward fold. Take two inches of range in the wall angel. Put your hands higher up your shin in the figure-4.

The value is in moving a joint through whatever range you have, under control, several times a day. A person with tight hamstrings who does that gets the benefit. A flexible person who sits for nine hours does not.

Can I do this in work clothes?

Yes. That was the design constraint, not an afterthought. Every routine was built around slacks or a skirt, a button-down, and shoes you did not choose for comfort. Four of the five moves in each routine are done seated or standing at the desk.

The only floor move anywhere on the site is the glute bridge, and it carries a standing substitute: brace against the desk and squeeze your glutes hard for five seconds, ten times. If your office has carpet and a door, the floor version is better. If it does not, the standing version is fine.

What if my back already hurts?

Ordinary, achy, stiff-from-sitting back pain usually tolerates gentle movement better than it tolerates staying still, and most of these moves are small. Start with less range than you think you need. Stop any move that sharpens the pain, and skip it next time.

There are symptoms that mean stop and get evaluated rather than stretch: numbness or tingling running down a leg, weakness in a leg or foot, pain that started with a fall or a crash, pain that wakes you at night, or any loss of bladder or bowel control, which is an emergency. Standing desk — better or not?

Better as an option. Worse as a religion. People who buy a standing desk and then stand for eight hours generally trade low back stiffness for foot, knee and hip complaints within a few weeks.

The gain is in the switching. Sit for a stretch, stand for a stretch, sit again. If you have a fixed-height desk, a monitor riser and a stack of books will get you a passable standing position for calls, which is the cheapest version of the same benefit. More on this in the desk setup guide.

How often should I do this?

Twice on a workday: late morning and mid-afternoon. If you can only manage one, take the afternoon one, when stiffness usually peaks and your setup has quietly degraded.

Separately from the routines, aim to change position roughly every half hour. That number is not magic — it is just short enough that you never settle in and long enough that you can still get work done. The CDC's activity guidance is about the week as a whole, and these breaks are not a substitute for it, but they are what fits inside a workday.

Stop and see a doctor instead if

You have numbness or tingling running down a leg, weakness in a leg or foot, pain that began after a fall or a car accident, or pain that wakes you at night. Those are not stiffness-from-sitting symptoms and stretching is not the answer to them. Loss of bladder or bowel control with back pain is a medical emergency — call 911.
