Why sitting hurts, and how to set up a desk

Most desk pain is not caused by bad posture. It is caused by one posture, held for eight hours. Here is what to change about the furniture, and the part that matters more than the furniture.

What sitting actually does

Sitting is not dangerous. Sitting still for a very long time is the problem, and it is worth being precise about why, because the precision changes what you do about it.

Three things happen over a workday. First, your hips stay folded at roughly 90 degrees. The muscles at the front of the hip spend the day short, and your glutes spend the day switched off and sat on. When you stand up, your pelvis is pulled into a tilt the low back has to work around, which is why standing up after a long meeting produces a specific and familiar wince.

Second, your spine settles into a long, low-effort round. Nobody holds an upright spine for eight hours; the muscles that would do it fatigue in minutes, so you sink into your ligaments and let the structure hold you. That is efficient and it is what everyone does. It is also a static load, and static load in one direction is what tissue complains about.

Third, your head drifts forward toward the screen. A head weighs ten to twelve pounds. The further forward it sits, the more work the muscles across the back of your neck and the top of your shoulders do to keep it there, without a break, all afternoon. That is the band of ache that shows up around three o'clock, and it is a workload problem rather than a weakness problem.

None of that is an injury. It is a load pattern. The reason the fix is movement rather than a better chair is that a load pattern is defined by time, and the only thing that changes time under load is getting up.

Why “sit up straight” is bad advice

It is not that upright sitting is bad. It is that upright sitting as an instruction fails in about ninety seconds, and then it fails worse than doing nothing, because you have spent effort and learned that you cannot do it.

Watch anyone told to sit up straight. They pull the shoulders back, arch the low back, lift the chest, and hold their breath slightly. That is a braced position, not a resting one. It costs muscular effort every second, it compresses the low back more than a neutral slouch does, and it lasts until the next email. Then the person sinks back down, and now they also feel like they failed at posture.

The advice that survives contact with a real workday is different: change position often. Slouch for a while. Sit upright for a while. Perch on the front of the chair. Lean back and let the backrest carry you. Stand. Sit on one hip, then the other. Every one of those is a fine position for twenty minutes and a bad one for four hours.

OSHA's own workstation guidance takes this line — there is no single correct posture, and the useful goal is variety within a comfortable range. Once you accept that, the whole setup question changes. You are not building a chair position to hold all day. You are building a desk that makes several positions available and none of them punishing.

The practical version

Stop trying to hold a good posture. Set the furniture so that the position you fall into is not terrible, and then change position before your body asks you to. The five-minute routines exist to give that change a shape, so it happens on purpose rather than when the ache reminds you.

Monitor height and distance

The single highest-return change most people can make, and it usually costs nothing.

Height. The top of the screen should sit at or just below eye level when you are looking straight ahead. Your eyes then rest naturally on the upper third of the display and your head does not need to tip forward or down. If you wear progressive lenses, go lower — you will otherwise tip your head back to find the reading window, which is the same problem upside down.

Distance. About an arm's length. Reach out and you should nearly touch the glass. Bigger screens want a little more distance, not less. If you find yourself leaning in to read, the answer is a larger font, not a closer monitor. Bump the system text size up a step and stop leaning.

Angle and position. Directly in front of you, not off to the side. A monitor placed at 30 degrees to one side means your neck spends the day rotated a little, which is invisible and expensive. Tilt the screen back slightly, five to ten degrees, so the surface faces your eyes.

Laptops. A laptop cannot satisfy both rules at once: if the screen is at the right height, the keyboard is too high. There are exactly two fixes and both are cheap. Put the laptop on a riser or a stack of books and use an external keyboard and mouse, or dock it to a separate monitor. A $25 keyboard fixes more neck pain than most things people spend $250 on.

Chair depth, height, and the back of your knees

Depth is the setting nobody adjusts. With your back against the backrest, you want two to three fingers of gap between the front edge of the seat and the back of your knees. Too deep and you cannot use the backrest without the seat pressing into your calves, so you perch forward and lose the back support entirely. Too shallow and your thighs are unsupported. Many office chairs have a seat-slide lever under the front right of the seat pan. Most people have never touched it.

Height comes before everything else. Set it so your thighs are roughly parallel to the floor and your feet are flat, then set the desk and monitor around that. If your desk is fixed height and too tall, raise the chair until your arms work and then solve your feet with a footrest. Do not lower yourself to meet a tall desk and spend the day with your shoulders up around your ears.

Backrest and lumbar support. The backrest should meet the curve of your low back, not the middle of your ribcage. If the chair has a height-adjustable lumbar pad, set it at belt level. If it does not, a rolled towel or a small cushion works and costs nothing. And then — this is the part people miss — actually lean on it. A lumbar support you never touch because you sit on the front edge of the chair is decoration.

Armrests. Set them so your shoulders are relaxed and your forearms rest lightly, with elbows near your sides at about 90 degrees. If the armrests force your shoulders up, drop them or take them off. Armrests that are too high are a common and completely invisible cause of upper trap ache.

Recline is allowed. Leaning back 10 to 20 degrees with the backrest supporting you is a legitimately good position and it takes load off the low back. It is not lazy. Alternate it with upright work through the day.

Feet flat, and what to do if they don't reach

Feet flat on the floor, ankles roughly under the knees, weight spread across both. That is it. The reason it matters is not virtue — it is that dangling feet put the whole weight of your lower legs on the front edge of the seat, and hooking your feet around the chair base rotates your pelvis and loads one side of your back all day.

If your feet do not reach the floor once the chair is at the right height for your arms, you need a footrest. A commercial one is about $25. A ream of copy paper is free and works. A closed laptop box, a phone book if your office still has one, two reams stacked — any stable flat object of the right thickness is fine.

The crossed-leg habit is worth naming. Crossing at the knee for a few minutes is harmless; doing it for six hours means one hip sits higher than the other for six hours. If you always cross the same way — most people do — alternate, or better, uncross and put both feet down for a while. The hip routine exists partly because of this habit.

The 20-8-2 rule

The most useful thing on this page is not a measurement. It is a rhythm, and it goes: for every half hour of work, spend roughly 20 minutes seated, 8 minutes standing, and 2 minutes moving.

It is a rule of thumb from the ergonomics world rather than a clinical prescription, and the exact numbers are not the point. What the ratio does well is make the target concrete. “Move more” is unactionable. “Twice an hour, stand up for eight minutes and walk for two” is something you can put on a calendar.

What it looks like in a real day:

  • Sit for the focused work. Twenty minutes is about one solid block of writing or code.
  • Stand for the things that do not need precision — a call, reading a document, triaging email. Eight minutes goes fast.
  • Walk for two. Fill the water bottle, take the long way to the restroom, walk one lap of the floor. This is the part everyone skips and the part that does the most.

If you cannot stand at your desk, the ratio still works with a smaller change: sit for twenty, get out of the chair for the next ten in whatever form is available. The change of position is the active ingredient, not the standing specifically.

One warning about timers and reminder apps: they work for about two weeks and then you start dismissing them without reading. Tying the break to something that already happens works better. Stand up every time a meeting ends. Walk every time you refill coffee. Run a five-minute reset before your standing 3 PM. Habits attached to existing events outlive notifications.

Standing desks, honestly

A sit-stand desk is a good purchase and a bad identity. The gain comes from switching, and the failure mode is picking a side. People who stand for eight hours reliably show up a few weeks later with foot, knee or hip complaints, having traded one static posture for another.

If you get one, use it in blocks. Stand for calls and reading, sit for detailed work. Two to four hours of standing spread across a day is plenty for most people, and none of it should be continuous.

Things that make standing work: an anti-fatigue mat, shoes that are not dress shoes, and moving your feet. Standing perfectly still on a hard floor is genuinely uncomfortable and there is no virtue in enduring it — shift your weight, put one foot up on a low rail or a box, change sides.

The setup rules do not change when you stand. Elbows at about 90 degrees, screen top at or just below eye level. Most people set their standing height too low and end up hunching, which is the seated problem transferred upward. If your desk has memory presets, set them once with a coworker checking your elbow angle and never think about it again.

If you do not have a sit-stand desk, you do not need to buy one to get the benefit. A monitor riser, a shelf, a filing cabinet, or a kitchen counter for one call a day gives you the change of position, which was the point.

The ten-minute setup pass

Do this once, today, in this order. Order matters — each step depends on the one before it.

  1. Chair height. Feet flat, thighs about parallel to the floor. Ignore the desk for now.
  2. Chair depth. Back against the backrest, two to three fingers behind the knees. Find the slide lever if there is one.
  3. Lumbar support. At belt level, meeting the curve of your low back. Rolled towel if the chair has nothing.
  4. Armrests. Shoulders relaxed, elbows near your sides at about 90 degrees. Lower them if they lift your shoulders.
  5. Keyboard and mouse. Close enough that your elbows stay near your sides. Wrists straight, floating rather than resting on a hard edge. Mouse beside the keyboard, not out past it.
  6. Monitor. Top of the screen at or just below eye level, arm's length away, squarely in front. Books work.
  7. Feet. Still flat? If they are dangling, put a ream of paper under them.
  8. Movement. Put two reminders in your calendar — 10:45 AM and 2:45 PM Eastern, or whatever your equivalent is — and attach a routine to each.

Then leave it alone. Setups get fiddled with endlessly by people who would get more from standing up twice an hour. The furniture is a floor, not a solution: it stops you being punished for sitting, and the movement is what actually changes how you feel at six o'clock.

When it isn't the desk

Setup and movement handle ordinary stiffness. They do not handle numbness or tingling running down a leg, weakness in a leg or foot, pain that started after a fall or a car accident, or pain that wakes you at night. Those need a doctor, not a monitor riser. Loss of bladder or bowel control with back pain is an emergency — call 911. See the health disclaimer.
